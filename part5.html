Problem 17: An Improved Prepayment Model

Having constructed forward curves, we return to prepayment. At a high level, borrower prepayments are influenced by two primary drivers:

  1. Refinance Incentive (Refi): Borrowers refinance when market rates drop, allowing a new loan at a lower rate. This is highly sensitive to rate changes.
  2. Demographics (Demo): Life events — job relocation, retirement, family changes, downsizing — also drive prepayments. These are less rate-sensitive and more tied to the borrower’s circumstances and the loan’s age.

Primary Current Coupon Model

The mortgage rate from the market is known as the Primary Current Coupon (PCC). To project it into the future, we use a simple model:

\[ PCC(t) = \text{DiscountRate}(t) + \text{PccSpread} \]

where \(\text{DiscountRate}(t)\) is the short rate and PccSpread is a fixed spread accounting for additional costs. Use 3.3% as your spread.

Refi Model

The refinance component is:

\[ \text{refi}(\text{spread}(t)) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } \text{spread} < 0 \\ \text{slope} \cdot \text{spread} & \text{if } 0 \leq \text{spread} \leq 0.015 \\ 0.0425 & \text{if } \text{spread} > 0.015 \end{cases} \]

where:

  • \(\text{slope} = \dfrac{0.0425}{0.015}\)
  • \(\text{spread}(t) = \text{GrossCoupon} - PCC(t)\)

Demographic Model

Demographic prepayments depend on loan age and seasonal trends:

\[\text{demo}(t) = \text{seasoning}(\text{age}(t)) \cdot \text{seasonal}(\text{month\_of\_year}(t))\]

  • Seasoning: Prepayments ramp linearly from 0 to a base SMM over the first 18 months. Use 0.5% as your base SMM.
  • Seasonality: Rates exhibit seasonal trends with summer peaks and winter dips. Use the monthly scalars below.

Combined Prepayment Model

\[SMM(t) = \text{refi}(\text{spread}(t)) + \text{demo}(t)\]

where \(t\) is time in months from the first of the market close’s month (e.g., if market close is 1/15/2024, then \(t\) is measured from 1/1/2024).

Problem 18: Negative Convexity

You may have noticed that the owner of this MBS loses more money when the market moves against them than they gain when it moves in their favor. For example:

2024-10-01 −0.5% 101.347358 +1.371574 3.23

In the fixed-income world, this is known as Negative Convexity. It arises because the MBS owner is effectively long a fixed-income amortizing bond while being short an American option that allows for prepayment.

Problem 19: Derivatives

The Taylor series gives us a second-order approximation to the change in MBS value given a change in rates.

The first derivative with respect to rates is the Dollar Value of an 01 (DV01). These exercises use 1% as the shift amount.

\[DV01 = \frac{V(\text{up}) - V(\text{dn})}{2 \cdot \text{shock}}\]

The second derivative is convexity (or gamma):

\[\text{Convexity} = \frac{V(\text{up}) + V(\text{dn}) - 2 \cdot V(\text{mid})}{\text{shock}^2}\]

where \(V(\text{mid})\) is the base value (shock = 0).

Problem 20: Multi-Path Valuation

Running our MBS along the rates from the step function above, your bootstrapped curve, and your smoother curve will each produce a different value. Worse, none individually captures the value of the optionality embedded in the security. We want to run a large number of possible trajectories through time and compute value as the average of the per-trajectory values.

Write a function that takes a vector of dates, a matrix of discount rate trajectories, and computes the value of our standard MBS with OAS of 3%. For this problem, your paths are: the original rates, a +50bp shock, and a −50bp shock. Although there are only three paths here, write your code to handle an arbitrary number.
